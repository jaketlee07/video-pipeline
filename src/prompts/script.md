## Structure

Let the brief decide the length. The narrator reads about 170 words a minute. A brief with several real cases and a long timeline can carry 2,800 to 3,800 words (about 16 to 22 minutes). A thinner one should stay around 2,000 to 2,400 (12 to 14 minutes). Never pad.

Tell it as a story, not a list of facts.

1. Hook (at most about 30 seconds, roughly 85 words): prove the title's promise within the first two sentences, and put the story's biggest stake in the hook rather than saving it (if a city went bankrupt, say so here). End on that stake or on the question the video answers, never on an expectation nobody really holds. No greeting, no channel intro, no "in this video". Pick the opening that fits the brief best:
   - a scene: one real place on a date, told in concrete detail ("On January 28, 2016, the Walmart in Fairfield, Alabama locked its doors for the last time.")
   - someone in a real place: what an ordinary person there runs into, step by step
   - the thing that should have happened but didn't: state the obvious expectation, then that reality went the other way
   - a number with a twist: a surprising statistic, then the short sentence that reframes it
   - a golden age: how good it was, in sensory detail, before the turn
2. Setup (up to about 1:30): why this matters to the viewer personally, and the video's argument in one plain sentence. Say it as part of the story, never announce it ("here's the argument of this video"), and don't list the places or chapters coming up.
3. How the money works: early on, walk through who pays whom with one concrete example from the brief's money flows, step by step, so a diagram can follow along.
4. Four to six chapters that move forward in time or through the event, each built around named people, companies or places and a turn where something changes. Follow one main case through the video, with two or three others for contrast; a place mentioned once in passing confuses more than it adds. Save the main case's biggest turn for the second half. Each lands one reveal backed by a number, a real case, or a quote from the brief's Quotes and documents. Open each chapter with a fresh question or an unresolved thread that pulls the viewer forward ("But the store closing wasn't what nearly bankrupted the city."), and pay it off before the chapter ends.
5. Takeaway: the non-obvious conclusion.
6. Tease: one or two sentences pointing to a related video.

Name who made the decisions and who benefited. When the evidence shows a company's or person's choice caused the outcome, say so plainly; never invent a villain the evidence doesn't support.

## Voice

Write like a sharp, curious friend explaining something they just figured out over a drink: conversational, a little wry, confident about what the evidence shows. Prefer concrete detail to abstraction: name the places, companies and years.

Let sentences flow the way people talk. Most should run 12 to 25 words, joined with "so", "which meant", "and that's because" or "but", so the thought carries from one to the next. Save very short sentences for punchlines and reveals, a few per chapter; a string of short sentences sounds robotic when a synthetic voice reads it.

Sound like a person thinking out loud:
- Ask the question the viewer is about to ask, then answer it straight away ("So why didn't the city just raise taxes? Because it legally couldn't."). Do this a few times in a video, not as every chapter's opener.
- Make the occasional aside or dry remark, and use an everyday comparison when a number or mechanism is abstract.
- Use contractions, and speak to the viewer as "you" when it's about their own life. The narrator can say "we" and "let's" as the channel ("let's go back to 2006"), but never invents personal experiences.

Don't repeat yourself. Every one of these makes a script sound templated:
- State the video's argument twice at most, in the setup and the takeaway. Each chapter adds something new instead of restating it.
- Explain each mechanism once. When a later chapter needs it again, point back to it in a phrase.
- Vary how chapters open, and start only a handful of sentences in the whole script with "So".
- Don't announce what's coming ("Here's the thing", "Here's where it gets interesting"); just say it.

Keep claims the size of the evidence. Say "almost never", "nobody" or "always" only when the brief gives a rate; otherwise say what happened in the cases shown. Check that later lines don't contradict earlier ones, such as calling a place hopeless after showing it recover.

Let numbers land: round them for the ear and say what they mean. Avoid YouTube filler such as "In today's video", "let's dive in", "buckle up" and "smash that like button".

## Pauses

Before the one or two biggest reveals in a chapter, give the narrator a beat: end the paragraph, and start the reveal's paragraph with [pause] on the same line as its first sentence, like "[pause] Four years later, the city filed for bankruptcy." Use [long pause] instead for the single biggest moment in the video. Use [pause] nowhere else and never mid-paragraph. Most chapters need one at most, and the takeaway's final line can have one too.

## Written for a text-to-speech narrator

- Wherever there's any ambiguity, write numbers, money and units the way they should be spoken: "about 2.3 billion dollars", "40 percent", "the 1990s".
- Leave symbols (such as %, $, & and ~), abbreviations, parentheses and stage directions out of the narration. The [pause] markers above are the only exception.
- Use only facts that are in the brief, and don't add numbers it doesn't contain. Where the brief marks a fact with ⚠️, either leave it out or state the uncertainty.
- Quote a source's exact words only from the brief's Quotes and documents, and say who said or wrote them. The quote will appear on screen as the narrator reads it.

## Point of view

The script should be complete and ready to read as it stands. Give it an editorial voice: react to what the evidence shows, weigh competing explanations, and end on a clear position that the brief supports. Speak as the channel, not as a person with a life story, so no first-person anecdotes.

## A question for viewers

Pick the question in this video that viewers are most likely to argue about: a judgment call the evidence leaves open, with good arguments on both sides, that the takeaway doesn't settle. Once the evidence for it has been laid out, have the narrator put it to viewers in one or two sentences and ask them to say what they think in the comments, without answering it or leaning it toward one side. Ask only once in the script.

## Format

Inside <script> tags, write markdown. Give each part a ### heading: ### Hook, ### Setup, a short name for each chapter (the money explainer counts as one), ### Takeaway and ### Tease. Under each heading, write the narration as plain paragraphs, with no bullet points, bold or links. Keep paragraphs to two to five sentences.

Then, inside <pinned_comment> tags, write a comment from the channel to pin under the video. It asks that question and one or two others the video raises, briefly and conversationally, and invites viewers to reply. Keep it to a few short lines of plain text, with no hashtags, links or emoji, and mention nothing that isn't in the video.
