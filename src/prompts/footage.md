Pick the footage for each scene of the video "{{title}}".

Each scene below says what should be on screen, the narration it plays under and, for real places, where. After it comes a still from each candidate: a video clip or a photo (photos get a slow pan and zoom), with the clip's length and its uploader's tags, or the photo's title. A Wikimedia Commons or Internet Archive video comes as a sheet of numbered stills instead, one from each uninterrupted stretch of it long enough for the scene; to use it, give the candidate number and the number of the still whose stretch fits (only that stretch plays). For each scene, pick the candidate that fits best:
- It shows what the scene describes, or something close enough that a viewer won't notice the difference.
- A photo of the actual place, store, company or person the narration names beats generic footage. When candidates fit equally well, prefer video, since it moves.
- It never passes off a different place or business as the one named. A photo titled with another town or store is fine only if the narration is general, not about that specific one.
- For a stock scene, which shows something ordinary rather than a named place, a video clip that fits beats a photo, since the video needs movement; take a photo only when no clip fits. A photo of some other town's store is not "the actual place".
- For an aerial scene, pick a photograph of the real place named, or nothing. A map, plan, census sheet or other scanned document is not a photograph of the place.
- It fits the place in the story. When the narration is about an American town, avoid footage that's plainly from somewhere else, such as European streets, foreign signage or cars driving on the left.
- It doesn't contradict the narration: no identifiable store or brand other than the one being named, no readable text that says something else, and nothing upbeat under a grim line.
- Prefer real, natural-looking footage to staged, animated or heavily stylized clips.
- Internet Archive candidates are old film (the year is given). Use one only when the narration is about that time or plainly looks back on it, and the still shows what the narration describes, not just something from the same film; its catalog description covers the whole film, not that still. Under a line about today, old film misleads. Never a title card, credits, a map or diagram, a narrator or actor speaking to the camera, or a staged scene with actors acting out a story.
- Real video of the actual place or company, even old or grainy (a store's opening day, a drive past the mall), is the best footage there is for a scene about it. From a news report, take a stretch of the place itself: never a person talking to the camera or being interviewed, a reporter, or a stretch with captions, a channel logo or other on-screen text.

A wrong pick is worse than none. If none of a scene's candidates would work, answer 0 for that scene, and it gets a graphic instead.
