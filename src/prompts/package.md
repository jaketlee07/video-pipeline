Write the packaging for the video "{{title}}" on Everyday Case Study, a YouTube channel of case studies on how the world really works, from a small-town store closing to a company in the news: the title and thumbnail a viewer sees before clicking, and the description under the video. Its titles are questions, and its thumbnails name the subject over a bright, real picture of it.

## Titles

Write five, best first. Each is the question the video answers, naming the concrete thing (the store, the town, the company), the way a curious person would ask it: "Why Does Every Highway Exit Have the Same Four Restaurants?" or "What Happens to a Company When Its Founder CEO Leaves?", not "The Economics of Highway Exits". Start with What, Why or How where it reads naturally, and end with a question mark. The video must answer it. At most 70 characters, ideally under 60. No number the video doesn't say, no "shocking" or "insane", no all caps.

## Thumbnails

Write three different ideas. Someone scrolling past sees it for a second, at the size of a matchbox: in that glance they should know what the video is about and want to know more. The best explainer channels do it with a topic across the top over a bright picture of the thing itself, and one callout that sets up the story ("THE ECONOMICS OF BUFFETS" over a heaped buffet tray; a first-class seat tagged "$10,000" beside an economy seat tagged "$500"). Each idea has:

- A headline across the top that names what the video is about, in two to four words: the subject, not the answer ("WHEN WALMART LEAVES", "THE DEATH OF XBOX", "PRIVATE EQUITY GYMS"). At most 22 characters. A kicker, a few small words above it ("THE ECONOMICS OF"), only when the headline needs it; otherwise leave it empty. Pick the one headline word to color.
- One picture filling the frame, or two side by side to set one thing against another: the company and the town, what one side lost and what the other did, before and after. Choose from the numbered images below, which come from the video: real photos of places in the story, and aerial views, where the site gets circled in pen. Prefer the brightest, most recognizable pictures, the ones that show the subject itself (the store with its sign on it, the building, the product), over moody or abstract ones. Say where each photo's subject is.
- One callout that hints at the answer or the stakes, so the viewer wants to know how. Either a tag on a picture, a big number or word ("<1%", "33%+", "BANKRUPT") with a few words under it saying exactly what it is ("of Walmart's revenue"); with two pictures, tag both so they read as a contrast. Or, with one picture, a receipt whose one circled line is the number that matters: what the receipt is ("City sales tax"), the item and its value, and an optional one-word stamp across it.

A number or claim must be exactly as strong as the narration makes it: where it gives a range or an estimate, use the range or its low end ("20%+", not "30%", when it says "an estimated 20 to 30 percent"), keep what a number measures exactly as the narration says it (a share of sales tax is not a share of all taxes), and don't state as fact what it says sources disagree on. Side by side, two things must not suggest a cause the narration doesn't claim. The picture should show what the words are about, and never put a place's name over a photo of somewhere else.

## Description

Two or three short paragraphs that retell the story: the places and companies by name, what happened, and why it matters to the viewer. Plain sentences. No hashtags, no emoji, no "in this video", and don't give away the ending's best line.

Then a chapter title for every part of the script below, two to six words, the way a viewer scanning the chapters would want them; the hook and setup can share one opening chapter.
