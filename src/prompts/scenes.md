## Scene types

- flow: an animated diagram of money, goods or customers moving between two to five named parties, one move at a time as the narration explains it. Use it whenever the narration walks through who pays whom, where the tax goes, or what gets blocked. Fill in `flow`: the parties, then the moves in the order the narration mentions them. Amounts only where the brief gives them, and two amounts in one diagram only when the brief itself compares them.
- document: a real source on screen, like a court record, filing, company statement, deed, law, study or news report, for when the narration cites it or quotes it. Fill in `document`. A quote must be the exact words from the brief, and only words the brief shows in quotation marks; otherwise leave the quote out. The heading is the source's own title, exactly as the brief's Sources list gives it, never a summary of your own (the card looks like the real source, so viewers must be able to find it). When the narration's point isn't in the title, the quote or the narration carries it.
- chart: an animated bar or line chart. Use only data that appears in the brief, with the values and source exactly as given there. If the data isn't in the brief, pick another type. Chart only exact values: when the brief gives a bound or an estimate ("less than 1%", "about 150"), use a number or a title instead. Give it a headline of one or two words that names what the data shows (like "UPSTAGED", "BURNOUT" or "TAX CLIFF"), and a title that says plainly what's plotted.
- number: one big number with a short label, for a stat the narration lands on. When the narration sets two numbers against each other ("it brought in X but needed Y"), use a chart or a flow instead.
- map: a real place highlighted on a map
- aerial: the camera flies down onto a real place from above, using US aerial imagery, ending on the town or on one named site in it (a mall, a road, a plant), which gets circled. Use it to show where something happened and what's there now. If the place can't be found, a photograph of it is used instead.
- title: a short text card for a section opener, a key line, or the question when the narrator asks viewers to comment. After a stock, image or aerial scene, a key line is shown in big words over that shot.
- stock: b-roll footage from Pixabay. Use it only for ordinary, visible things the narration describes (a parking lot, a checkout line, a bus stop), never as a metaphor for an idea (a gavel for "the law", a handshake for "a deal"): show the document, a flow or a title instead.
- image: real imagery of a company, store or person, sourced later from press kits or Wikimedia Commons. Never ask for AI-generated imagery of real brands or people.

For every scene, also give two or three Pixabay search queries as fallback b-roll. Pixabay matches the words against the tags uploaders give their clips, so make each one or two common words naming something stock videographers often film (like "parking lot", "shopping cart" or "main street"), not detailed descriptions (like "weeds in cracked parking lot") or abstract ideas (like "economic decline").
